# Writing criteria for this guide

Every "for Mere Mortals" guide is written to the same nine-point
standard, so a reader who trusts one can trust the rest. Here it
is, with the verification notes for this particular guide below.

1. **Fix it first.** Reader outcomes lead, not action items: each
   chapter opens by naming the outcome it delivers, shows a check
   the reader can run to confirm they got it, and a reasonable
   reader finishes able to state what was done, what it means,
   and what to try next.
2. **Plain language.** Short sentences, everyday vocabulary,
   technical terms defined on first use. No slang, and no
   idioms that only make sense in one country — the reader may
   be translating as they go.
3. **No dead ends.** A guide never tells the reader to do
   something it cannot show them how to verify. Every claim a
   reader relies on — an install, a command, an output — is
   something this guide demonstrates or clearly labels
   documented-only below.
4. **Live-verified where it matters.** Procedures are run for
   real in the guide's lab wherever the build machine allows it,
   and the transcripts kept in the repository are the evidence.
   What could not be run is listed honestly below, never
   presented as if it had been.
5. **General audiences.** Nothing in this guide — examples,
   fixtures, names, addresses — is modelled on any particular
   reader or author's real life, network, or organisations.
   The lab's pages are pages the lab invented and printed
   itself.
6. **Standalone.** A reader needs this guide, its lab, and
   nothing else. Where another guide would genuinely help, it
   is pointed to once, by name, and the chapter still completes
   without it.
7. **Honest about limits.** Chapter 14 names what the tool
   cannot do: handwriting, meaning and arithmetic, the
   confident error, damage that destroys characters outright,
   tables and forms that scatter, and the languages and
   scripts whose data is thin.
8. **The ethics rule is in the text, early.** Chapter 1 and the
   README state the rule plainly — *read what is yours, or
   what you have explicit permission to read* — before any
   procedure depends on it, and later chapters point back to
   it where the tooling would make crossing the line easy.
9. **Builds clean.** The site builds with MkDocs in strict mode
   with no warnings, and a fresh reader following the chapters
   in order reaches the stated outcome of each.

## Verification notes for this guide

The build machine is a small Linux sandbox (Ubuntu 24.04).
Tesseract **5.3.4** with Leptonica 1.82.0 was run from Ubuntu's
own packages (`tesseract-ocr` 5.3.4-1build5; language packs
`tesseract-ocr-eng`, `-deu`, `-fra` and the `osd` data at
4.1.0-2). One deviation, stated plainly: the sandbox's package
manager was held by other system work for the entire build
(direct installs were attempted and refused on the dpkg lock),
so the `.deb` files were fetched from the Ubuntu archive and
unpacked into a scratch folder, and the packaged binary was
run from there. Same binary, same version, same data files as
`apt install tesseract-ocr` delivers; every transcript in the
lab was produced by it.

All fixtures are generated by the lab (`make-images.py`,
`make-images-hard.py`) from invented texts, with the true text
of every page kept beside its image, so every accuracy figure
in the guide is a measured **character error rate** from
`measure.py` (Levenshtein distance over whitespace-normalised
text). The full ledger is
`tesseract-lab/transcripts/t90-accuracy-ledger.txt`.

Run for real, with transcripts as evidence:

- Version and language listing of the packaged engine (t00).
- The clean notice read to screen and file, measured at 100.0%
  character accuracy (t01).
- Twelve damaged variants of that notice, each measured raw:
  skew 3° (99.6%) and 8° (38.0%); speckle mild (96.9%) and
  heavy (83.8%); pale ink at two levels (100.0% both); uneven
  light (100.0%) and a hard shadow (97.9%); about-100-DPI
  (99.6%) and about-70-DPI (87.5%) renderings; an image with
  no DPI metadata (100.0%, with Tesseract's resolution
  estimate reported); upside down (14.7%) (t02, t02b).
- The repairs, each measured after: deskew −8.00° to 100.0%;
  median filter plus threshold to 93.2%; 3× upscale to 99.2%;
  background normalisation plus threshold on the shadowed
  page, which scored 95.8% — worse than the raw page, and
  reported as such (t03).
- Page segmentation: the two-column article under modes 3, 4
  and 6 (6 and 4 interleave the columns); a single line under
  modes 3 and 7; a single word under modes 3 and 8 (mode 8
  failed twice, including on a bordered retry, and the text
  says so); the orientation report under mode 0 and the
  flipped page read upright at 100.0% under mode 1 (t04).
- The German notice under `-l eng` (99.2%, *darüber* misread),
  `-l deu` (100.0%) and `-l eng+deu` (99.2%) (t05).
- All four output formats from one page, with sizes; the
  searchable PDF's text layer extracted with `pdftotext` and
  measured at 98.1% against the plain text's 100.0%, the
  difference traced to the extractor's word order (t06).
- Word confidence from TSV: the wrong word *moming* at 59.6,
  junk tokens at 0, and a correct *morning* at 36.0 under
  speckle (t07).
- A folder batch by shell loop, a list-file read combining
  two pages into one output, and the stdin/stdout pipe form
  (t08). The bakery receipt's read measured 73.6% while a
  character diff showed the only differences were two dashed
  separator rules — every word and figure correct; the guide
  uses it to teach diff-before-number (t90).

Documented only — described from the project's documentation,
not run, and the text does not claim otherwise:

- The macOS and Windows installation routes (the lab is
  Linux-only); the packaged Linux route is described from the
  same packages the lab ran.
- Segmentation modes 2, 5, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13, listed
  for completeness; modes 0, 1, 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8 are the
  ones exercised.
- The `tessdata` and `tessdata_best` model flavours and
  hand-downloaded traineddata placement (the mechanics are
  shown; the lab's packs came from the archive identically).
- The neighbour tools and services of chapter 13, including
  OCRmyPDF and the cloud engines, characterised rather than
  benchmarked.
- Real-photograph damage (folds, bleed-through, glare) and
  historical print such as Fraktur, discussed from documented
  behaviour and the shape of the lab's synthetic results.
- Handwriting: not run because the engine does not claim it;
  chapter 14 says so rather than demonstrating a foregone
  failure.

Fixtures are this guide's own and deliberately generic: a
fictional community hall's cleanup notice, an invented bakery
receipt whose figures sum correctly, a two-column gardening
article, and a German reading-circle notice. No example is
modelled on any real person, organisation, document or
network, and every page OCR'd in the lab is a page the lab
itself created.
